# Message architecture

## The fold is a thesis, not a hero

The area a visitor sees before scrolling has to survive a three-second scan and
answer four questions. Anything else in that space is competing with them.

**Headline — what do I get?**

Write it from the mechanism, not from the brand. Three shapes that work:

- *Outcome*: "Ship a landing page you can defend"
- *Outcome with the constraint that makes it credible*: "Type-safe API clients,
  generated from your OpenAPI spec, in one command"
- *Named enemy*: "Stop paying for seats you do not use"

Two shapes that do not:

- The product name as the headline. The reader does not know it yet.
- A category label — "The modern platform for teams". Interchangeable, so it
  says nothing.

Test: cover the logo. If the headline could sit on a competitor's page
unchanged, it is not a headline yet.

**Subhead — who is it for, and how does it work?**

One or two sentences that make the headline checkable. This is where the
mechanism goes, and where the audience gets named so the wrong reader can leave
without resentment. A subhead that only restates the headline in longer words is
wasted space.

**Action — what do I do now?**

Present before the scroll. A reader who arrived already convinced must not have
to hunt.

**Signal — why believe any of this?**

One piece of evidence, not a wall. A single named customer beats eight grey
logos. If you have nothing yet, leave it out; an empty slot is better than an
invented one.

## Ordering sections against objections

The sequence is an argument. Each section removes one reason not to act, in the
order the reader raises them.

| Reader's objection | The section that answers it |
|---|---|
| I do not have this problem | Problem, stated in their words |
| I have it, but this will not work for my case | Mechanism, concrete enough to be doubted |
| It might work, but I do not believe *you* | Proof with attribution |
| I believe you, but it costs too much | Pricing, with the comparison made explicit |
| I can afford it, but switching is too much work | Migration, integrations, time to first value |
| All true, but not right now | A reason now is different from later, if one honestly exists |

Two things follow from this table:

1. **You cannot answer an objection the reader has not reached.** Pricing before
   mechanism reads as pressure.
2. **Sections without an objection attached are padding.** The three-feature-card
   row exists on most landing pages because it is easy to produce, not because
   anyone needed it.

If the brief says the main objection is trust, the proof section moves up and
gets longer, and the feature grid probably disappears. Let the objection order
drive the layout rather than filling a template.

---

## Length

Length is set by how many objections you have to clear, not by fashion. A
free tool with no risk attached needs a fold and a button. A five-figure annual
contract needs every row of that table.

Signs the page is too short: the reader has to guess how it works; a real
objection is never named. Signs it is too long: two sections answer the same
objection; a section exists because the layout looked unbalanced without it.

---

## Writing the copy

- **Specific beats clever.** "Deploys in 40 seconds" survives scrutiny;
  "lightning fast" invites it.
- **Second person, active voice.** The page is addressed to one reader.
- **Verbs the reader recognises.** Name things the way they would search for
  them, not the way the codebase does.
- **Cut every sentence that would still be true about a competitor.** That
  sentence is describing a category, not a product.
- **Read it aloud.** The place you run out of breath is where the sentence
  breaks in two.
