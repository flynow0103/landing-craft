# Proof, and the line you do not cross

## The rule

**Never invent proof.** No testimonial, customer name, company logo, review
count, user total, revenue figure, benchmark result, or certification that you
cannot point to a source for. Not as a mockup, not as a placeholder, not with
"replace before launch" in a comment.

This is not a conversion-rate argument. A fabricated testimonial is a false
statement of fact about a real person or company, presented to induce a
transaction. In most jurisdictions that is a consumer-protection problem, and
under the EU Unfair Commercial Practices Directive and the US FTC's rules on
endorsements it is squarely prohibited. It also has a habit of shipping: the
placeholder that was going to be replaced is the one that goes live.

If you are generating a page and the brief has no proof in it, the correct
output is a proof section that is empty, with a comment naming what belongs
there. Say so in the handoff. Do not fill it to make the page look finished.

The same applies to a design mockup shown to a client: label invented content as
invented, on the artboard, where it cannot be cropped away.

---

## What counts as proof, strongest first

1. **Something the reader can try.** A live demo, a sandbox, a free tier. Proof
   the reader generates themselves needs no trust.
2. **A number with its method.** "Median cold start 41 ms, measured over 10,000
   invocations on the smallest instance, March 2026." The method is what makes
   the number worth stating.
3. **An attributed customer statement.** Name, role, company. An unattributed
   quote is read as invented and drags down the claims near it.
4. **A named customer or logo you have permission to use.** Permission is not
   optional; check the contract.
5. **Third-party evidence.** Independent review, audit report, standards
   certification, published research. Link it.
6. **Volume claims.** "4,000 teams" is the weakest useful form, and only if the
   number is real and current.

Below that line: unnamed logos, star ratings with no source, "trusted by
industry leaders", and anything phrased to imply a scale you do not have.

## Handling the case where you have nothing

Early products have no proof, and pretending otherwise is both dishonest and
transparent. Things that legitimately substitute:

- **Show the work.** A public changelog, an open repository, a technical writeup
  of the hard part. Competence is evidence.
- **Be specific about who you are.** A real name, a real location, a way to
  reach a human. Anonymity is the thing most likely to stop a first purchase.
- **Reduce what you are asking for.** If you cannot yet prove the claim, ask for
  less: an email instead of a card, a demo instead of a contract.
- **Say what stage you are at.** "We launched three weeks ago and have eleven
  teams using it" is credible in a way that "trusted by teams worldwide" is not.

---

## Claims that need care

- **Comparisons with a named competitor.** Must be accurate, current, and
  like-for-like. Comparative advertising is legal in most places and actionable
  when it misleads. Date the comparison and say what version you tested.
- **Pricing.** Show what the reader will actually pay, including the billing
  period. A price that turns out to be per-seat-per-month at the checkout is a
  conversion win and a retention loss.
- **Guarantees and refunds.** Only state terms that are actually implemented.
- **Security and compliance.** "SOC 2" means the report exists. "Bank-level
  encryption" means nothing and reads as a bluff to anyone who would care.
- **AI claims.** Say what the model does and what it does not. Overclaiming here
  is currently the fastest way to lose a technical audience.

---

## A checklist before launch

- [ ] Every quote has a real name, role and company, used with permission
- [ ] Every logo is used with permission
- [ ] Every number has a method, a sample and a date somewhere on the page
- [ ] Every comparison is current and like-for-like
- [ ] No section exists purely to look substantial
- [ ] Anything invented for a mockup is labelled as invented
